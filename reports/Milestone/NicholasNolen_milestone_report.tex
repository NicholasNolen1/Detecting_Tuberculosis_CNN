\begin{abstract}
% A brief overview of the paper.

Tuberculosis is one of the world's most infectious and deadly diseases. Tuberculosis affects about 25\% of the world's population and is responsible for 
roughly 1.2 million deaths each year. In this work I investigate using a CNN model to assist health officials in the detection of Tuberculosis 
on sputum slides stained with the Ziehl-Neelsen microscopy technique. Through my methods I achieved a 0.89 positive prediction rate and a 0.41 false-positive prediction rate.
\end{abstract}

% --- Introduction ---
\section{Introduction}
% Describes the motivation of this work and outlines the rest of the paper.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) approximately a quarter of the world's population is 
currently infected with Tuberculosis. In addition, the disease has been shown to be much more likely to 
have extreme adverse health impacts and be much harder to cure for those living in poverty. The best way to
prevent the spread of Tuberculosis in these places is being able to diagnose the disease early, however most diagnostic tools are very
expensive and the people living in these communities do not have access to them.

The current most widespread diagnostic tool for Tuberculosis in impoverished areas is sputum smear microscopy. 
This is a diagnostic technique where a trained technician examines a sputum smear from a patient under a microscope
and looks for Tuberculosis bacteria. This method is time-consuming and has the potential to overlook between 33\%-55\% of cases.

Because of the low detection rate of the manual method, I have created a CNN image classification model which can be used
to assist lab technicians by finding $(244 \times 244)$ pixel image crops from a microscope viewpoint and sort the crops
by their likelihood of containing Tuberculosis Bacilli. This proposed method has the potential to greatly speed up the lengthy and error
prone process of manually detecting Tuberculosis Bacilli.

\section{Related Work}

A description of similar work in the area of using machine learning models for Tuberculosis Bacilli detection
on Ziehl-Neelsen sputum smears:

\textbf{Automatic bright-field smear microscopy for diagnosis of pulmonary Tuberculosis:}
The authors of this paper also used a CNN to detect Tuberculosis. They evaluated multiple architectures and even used
an encoder-decoder architecture as opposed to a generic CNN architecture, finding it to have better results.

While the authors of this paper had good success in training their model, I believe that my proposed methods are improvements
in that their crop size was much larger than mine at $(400 \times 400)$ rather than $(244 \times 244)$. Further, they only used 
one dataset for their images, choosing to strengthen their model through augmentation, while I plan to use multiple distinct
datasets in addition to augmenting my training images.


\textbf{Pulmonary Tuberculosis Diagnosis Using an Intelligent Microscopy Scanner and Image Recognition Model for Improved Acid-Fast Bacilli Detection in Smears:}
The authors of this paper were the ones who constructed the dataset the Wellgen Medical Subset was based off of. This study
constructed a CNN model using 100,000 sputum slides. Due to the large amount of input, their model was able to perform
very well, but because they used entire sputum slides as input there is potential that the model overfit some features. 

My method can potentially be an improvement on their method because, rather than use the entire slide as input, I extract crops which 
are easier for the CNN model to pull features out of and make inferences on. This reduces the size of my model and the amount of training
data necessary for me to get adequate results.

\section{Methodology}
% Describes what is the approach taken in this paper.

The main approach I used to train the CNN is to crop the dataset images from their starting resolution to a resolution
of $(244 \times 244)$ pixels around labeled Tuberculosis Bacilli. I then use this model trained on $(244 \times 244)$
sized images of Tuberculosis Bacilli to detect the presence of Tuberculosis Bacilli in $(244 \times 244)$ crops of any slide.

I am generating the crops using the following dataset and methods:

\textbf{ODS3 dataset:}
The Original Dataset 3(ODS3)\cite{Costa2025} is a dataset which was compiled using 3,280 extended depth focus bitmap images. If there are
Tuberculosis Bacilli present on the image there is a corresponding CSV with ``b'' or ``bc'' relating to a detected bacillus
or bacillus cluster. I used these CSVs to create $(244 \times 244)$ crops of the images which can be used to train the CNN model
at a closer resolution to better capture the features of the Tuberculosis bacteria. Creating these crops from the positively labeled
Tuberculosis Bacilli resulted in 10,897 positive images. I then compiled a similar number of negative $(244 \times 244)$ image crops by
cropping $(244 \times 244)$ images from slides without corresponding annotation files. I did this to get 4 crops per image which resulted
in 9,726 negative crops.


\textbf{TBDB dataset:}
The Tuberculosis Database (TBDB)\cite{TBDB} contains 600 $(612 \times 480)$ resolution images of Tuberculosis sputum smears along with
XML files containing bounding boxes detailing the location of the Tuberculosis Bacilli and Debris on the image. To make this data
compatible with my model architecture, I cropped out $(244 \times 244)$ segments from these images where there was a Tuberculosis
Bacillus labeled as present. This resulted in 1,482 positive image crops. In addition, to generate negative class images I 
made crops around the annotation locations which were labeled as Debris. 

As the crops generated from this dataset are slightly larger than the ones generated from the ODS3 dataset, it is useful
in training the model to detect Tuberculosis Bacilli of more varying sizes.


\textbf{Testing via Wellgen Medical Subset:}
The Wellgen Medical dataset\cite{lin2024tbdata} is a 1000 image subset of a large dataset of $(2448 \times 2048)$ Ziehl-Neelsen sputum smear slides. 
The slides in this dataset are labeled positive and negative, but none of the positive slides have bounds around the locations of the
Bacilli. Because of this I cannot use this dataset to train the CNN model. However, I can use this dataset to get an idea of how well my
model performs on data it hasn't been trained on.

Specifically, to test models using the images in this dataset I separate the $(2448 \times 2048)$ images into 80 (the maximum number possible)
$(244 \times 244)$ crops. I then have the model being tested predict the probability that Tuberculosis Bacilli are present in each of the crops and
if the probability is greater than a threshold I count the slide as being positive for Tuberculosis. I run this for the 100 positive slides
in the dataset and 100 of the negative slides in the dataset and then divide the number of positive labels for either slide by the number of
slides tested (100 slides). This results in a number representing the percent of slides labeled above the threshold out of the total number of slides.

The number of positive prediction labels can be called a positive prediction rate and the rate of the number of negative slides labeled positive can be 
interpreted as the false-positive prediction rate.


Ultimately this model is being trained to assist lab technicians in diagnosing Tuberculosis, so the average prediction is not as important as how
well the model is able to sort sputum crops based on their likelihood of containing Tuberculosis. However, the average positive and false-positive
predictions are still useful as a metric to how well the model performs to maximize its effectiveness in sorting.

\textbf{Drafting CNN Architecture to Minimize Loss:}
Using my initial CNN configuration I achieved a validation accuracy of 0.8264 and a validation loss of 0.4319.


While the accuracy is fairly high, a loss of 0.4319 shows that the model is uncertain about the validity of its predictions.

I increased the number of filters for the first convolutional layer to 64, this would hopefully
allow me to capture the shape of the small Bacilli more accurately from the beginning
I also lowered the kernel size from (3,3) to (1,1) to try and understand how the size of the kernel affects detection.

accuracy and loss have greatly improved. 

To further increase accuracy and decrease loss I tried the model with 128 filters on the first 
convolutional layer. This change resulted in a validation accuracy of 0.8938 and a validation loss of 0.3129.

\textbf{Optimizing Architecture Based on Test Set:}
While it is acceptable to use the validation set to search for an appropriate group of hypothesis classes, which in this
case it would be the structure of the network, it is not adequate to use the validation set to estimate the model's overall 
performance.

To approximate the classification power of the model I will be using the previously described test set of Ziehl-Neelsen slides which contains
images that none of the models were trained on. The accuracy threshold I will be using to evaluate my probabilities is 0.80

With my latest model I achieved a positive prediction rate of 0.53 and a false-positive prediction rate of 0.45.

These numbers show that the model is doing a very poor job at correctly detect Tuberculosis bacterium and major improvements still need
to be made before the model is viable.

The high testing accuracy and low real-world accuracy of my model lead me to believe that my data was overfitting. So, because simpler models 
are less prone to overfitting I reduced the amount of filters present in my model.
However, at the same time I increased the number of layers believing that with the additions of more layers my model will still able to 
fit the data well.

To further improve my model I will randomize my crops every 5 epochs, this will prevent overfitting based on location and will help the model learn

Also, I will augment the dataset with the TBDB dataset to help the model learn variations in size

Finally, I will add images with varying saturation, color, brightness, stretched the images, blur similar to the process that
Trilaksana et al. underwent with their model to further improve my model's generalization power.

\section{Discussion and Analysis}
% Examines the results of the experiments and draws some conclusions about their significance.
\textbf{Discussion:}
Detecting Tuberculosis via Ziehl-Neelsen microscopy is the cheapest and most accessible Tuberculosis diagnostic technique but very time-consuming and prone to false negatives. Because
of this, a tool that accurately flags potential Tuberculosis presence which can be reviewed by a technician
is highly useful. Such a tool could be operated relatively inexpensively but greatly increase the rate of
Tuberculosis detection and the speed of diagnosis by displaying crops with the highest probability of
Tuberculosis presence to help a technician narrow down potential sites of Tuberculosis activity quicker 
than if they were manually observing a sputum slide.

\textbf{Analysis:}
A 0.89 positive prediction rate is quite ideal. This rate shows that there is a high likelihood that crops with Tuberculosis Bacilli will be preferred
by the model to images without Bacilli. Conversely, a 0.41 false-positive prediction rate still needs to be improved. This result implies that almost half of the crops
tested by the model will be incorrectly labeled with a high likelihood that they contain Tuberculosis Bacilli.




% --- Conclusion ---
\section{Conclusion}
% Summarizes the paper.

The problem of detecting Tuberculosis is difficult because the places where it is most prevalent also lack adequate healthcare funding to do something about it.
The most accessible diagnostic tool in the poorer regions where Tuberculosis is highly infectious is Ziehl-Neelsen stained slide microscopy. This technique is 
cheap and therefore accessible, but requires trained technicians to spend hours looking through microscope lenses to diagnose a single patient. The classification
system proposed in this paper would be able to greatly speed up the rate at which a technician is able to classify Tuberculosis Bacilli by presenting areas of a
viewfield to the technician where there is the greatest likelihood of Tuberculosis being present.
